# AI Startup Fundraising

VC outreach increasingly looks like founder work itself. When Sean posted on X about receiving three a16z speedrun invitation emails (he had applied and signed up to their Substack with multiple addresses [00:59]), "the partner at A16Z Andrew Chen messaged me on Twitter on X. Their marketing partner Ryan also messaged me" [00:40]. Instead of hiding, "immediately they jump into the debugging mode" [01:10]. Sean reads this as a sign of the times: "it's really interesting that these days VCs behave like founders too" [01:23], and it doubles as a lesson - "this is what founders are supposed to do when your potential customers or existing customers are talking about your products publicly you should read them and engage with your customers" [01:31].

Demo days are where agent-native pitching happens. At SF Demo Day by Character Labs, Sean pitched AutoManus with a stunt: "I created 53 agents for every VC" [03:19], sent each investor a personalized agent link on LinkedIn or email, and opened the pitch by pointing to the audience. The pitch itself reads like a fundraising archetype: founder pedigree (MIT, Google, Walmart [00:34]), the problem (consultative sales, "60% are dropped because of missed follow-ups" [02:45]), the market ("a $30 billion opportunity" [02:54]), traction ("125 sign-ups from businesses with zero CAC because of my organic content. Seven ops in the pipeline and three main customers with $20,000 ARR" [04:33]), and the ask: "we're raising $3 million" [04:52].

The UK founders contrast London and San Francisco fundraising cultures sharply. Victoria summarizes: "America's more in conviction rather than proof and the UK's more in proof because we're so risk averse" [19:06]. In SF she met investors who shrugged off small raises as "less than my kitchen renovation" [18:53]. Sean's own story is the control experiment: Antler London rejected him while partners pushed projected revenue [19:26], and the US investors who later backed him said the opposite - "Why do you put the projected revenue there? You're a pre-seed company. Nobody's going to believe that projected revenue" [20:03]. UK investors "want to believe in the founder... but they also want you to have the proof" [22:16], a "launch before funding, funding before launch" paradox [22:31].

The videos also strip the mystique from investors. "Being an investor has no moat. Because you just need to have money" [55:21], and after YC's SAFE, "everybody can write a SAFE to anybody" [55:35]. Sean confesses his own early mistake: "I fantasize or even think investors are some holy figure or they're like just authorities... No, they're not. Sometimes they're very shitty people who just have some money" [55:52]. The practical rules that follow: investors are humans [48:31], "investors really appreciate honesty" [56:50], Canva "famously got rejected 100 times by 100 different VCs" [49:26], pick investors who contribute rather than just write checks (Dragon's Den style [58:00]), and "most relationships with investors last longer than marriages these days" [58:40].

Founder-led content is the cheapest fundraising channel in these videos. Sean's AutoManus gets inbound because "a lot of our customers actually inbound into my product because of my videos" [00:57] - a million organic views, 45,000 followers, and zero CAC. Jason Zhou raised his first round in the US on the back of a YouTube channel and an open-source project with 4,000 GitHub stars [16:26]. The ex-Kimi product lead generalizes it: content is the top of funnel, personal branding filters leads, and "familiarity itself can sometimes drive purchases" [31:41] - but that is a distribution story, covered under [AI Content Creation](/concepts/ai-content-creation.md).
