# AI Agent Startups

Leon Xiao Ming's trajectory is a compressed history of the agent wave. He joined ByteDance in 2020 on Jianying (CapCut overseas) [03:42], left after "Midjourney and ChatGPT exploded" [03:55], joined Kimi "at Kimi's darkest time" [04:16] and lived its "suddenly exploding in March 2024" [04:24]. The decisive trigger for his own venture was Sonnet 3.5: "its most important ability was... enabling ordinary people to code" [04:45], so "we resolutely left Kimi" [05:08] and raised "tens of millions. Over $40 million" [01:38] for two products: YouWare, "a vibe coding tool that appeared before Lovable" [01:45], and Bloome, "mainly for team collaboration, tools for invoking agents" [01:52]. The pivot logic is instructive: when everyone piled into coding ("everyone realized CODING is EVERYTHING" [05:41]) and "facing token subsidies from Anthropic and Codex you feel powerless" [05:47], the team turned an internal agent shell into Bloome [06:07].

The core thesis is human-agent collaboration by design. "From day one with Bloome, we treated agents as people" [24:21]. Tools like OpenClaw "only know you... they have only one owner" [24:33], whereas person-designed agents "exist within a network... they have their own social graph" [24:41], echoing Marx: "Man is the ensemble of social relations" [25:10]. Observed behavior in his own team: "zero human-to-human @mentions now" [21:53], and "nine out of ten messages @mention agents instead of people" [36:02], which "emerged organically" [36:11]. The payoff is "at least a 5x improvement" [23:20], with bug reports turning into "@CodingAgent: 'Fix this'" and "ten minutes later: Fixed" [22:19]. Organizationally this becomes permission management: "if you treat agents as people, then for an organization, it essentially becomes permission management" [27:48], because "every company is essentially a file system" [29:10]. Agents also solve institutional memory - "organizations composed solely of humans lack institutional memory" [26:01], and departing salespeople "take their notebooks with them" [26:18] - the territory of [Business Memory System](/concepts/business-memory-system.md) and [Agent Memory System](/concepts/agent-memory-system.md).

China vs Silicon Valley is drawn in sharp strokes. Both "grind hard" [07:36], but "Silicon Valley leans more towards enterprise. Building B2B products. Chinese teams focus more on consumers" [08:12]: over 20 years "tech giants emerged from consumer markets. None from B2B" [08:24], while America's last major consumer product was Snapchat [08:35]. ByteDance's distribution power shapes Chinese competition - "where Toutiao passes, not a blade of grass grows" [11:08], Douyin even blocked Kimi ads at its peak [12:24]. Hence his own products "target... global non-China markets" [10:42]: "America remains friendly to small startups. Very welcoming" [12:49], with liquidity where "a $500M or $1B US company could easily be acquired by Google, NVIDIA, Meta" [12:53]. Shenzhen meanwhile "focuses on hardware" [08:56] and now pulls "top-tier European and American investment firms... head straight to Shenzhen" [10:12].

The pricing thesis is the most radical. "Token-based pricing is inherently flawed" [37:37]: it is dock-worker piece-rate economics from his native Hubei [37:44], applied to a world where a million tokens spent proving the Riemann Hypothesis with Terence Tao are priced identically to a million tokens on tomato-and-egg recipes [38:48]. "Pricing must theoretically be outcome-based" [39:10], and "if everyone sticks to token-based pricing, no one will make money. Not even Anthropic" [39:22]. The platform layer will monetize trust instead - Taobao's escrow as the model [39:32], Stripe's checkout trust ("Stripe built a cult in Silicon Valley" [41:19]), and "Anthropic... acquire consulting firms essentially to backstop their agents with trust and service guarantees" [40:23]. Today "AI bears no liability for outcomes" [40:04]; whoever absorbs liability wins.

His pragmatic cautions complete the picture. Adoption blockers are not technical ("technical challenges are generally solvable" [33:34]) but inertial: "the primary obstacle is human nature" [34:24], and IM history shows Slack-like tools win with "early adopters with zero legacy baggage" [35:03]. On strategy, Kuaishou's retired CEO Su Hua told him: "First build a castle, only then does a moat matter. Don't dig moats on day one" [46:45]. And even agent-native marketing keeps a human core: "field sales will never disappear" [32:57], a content-funnel view detailed in [AI Content Creation](/concepts/ai-content-creation.md).
