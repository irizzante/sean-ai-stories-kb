# I Interviewed the Ex-Kimi & CapCut Product Lead Who Raised $40M to Build AI Agents

Sean Chen interviews Leon (Xiao Ming) at a Shenzhen coffee-shop event Leon booked out for the occasion: former product lead at Kimi, previously at ByteDance working on Jianying (CapCut overseas), now running his own company after raising "over $40 million" [01:40] to build YouWare, a vibe-coding tool that predated Lovable, and Bloome, a team-collaboration tool where humans invoke agents [01:43]. His path: 2018 Shenzhen phones and hardware, 2020 ByteDance, then after 2023 he joined Kimi because "that was Kimi's darkest time" [04:16], riding its March 2024 explosion and subsequent controversies for a year. He resigned again around July/August when Sonnet 3.5 convinced him coding was no longer developer-only: while Cursor targeted developers for efficiency, "our immediate reaction was that this should target the larger non-developer market" [05:04]. Then Lovable launched in March 2025, now valued at $6.6B [05:14], everyone piled into coding agents within six months ("CODING is EVERYTHING" [05:41]), and "facing token subsidies from Anthropic and Codex, you feel powerless" [05:47] - so Bloome started as an internal side project for sharing Claude Code skills [05:52].

On the two ecosystems: Shenzhen and the Bay Area both grind hard, and "America's absolute top elites, their intensity is terrifying" [07:44]; Silicon Valley leans enterprise B2B while China leans consumer ToC, with Snapchat as basically the only major US consumer product in 20 years [08:35]. "Shenzhen is attracting global capital and talent" [10:21], with US institutions now required to visit to see Chinese robotics/AI companies. He goes global because China's competition is terrifying: ByteDance is "where Toutiao passes, not a blade of grass grows" [11:08], Douyin is a money printer that monopolized top graduates, and "during Kimi's peak ad spending, couldn't buy ads on Douyin" [12:24] - videos mentioning Kimi got shadowbanned instantly. The US has liquidity, acquisitions by Google/NVIDIA/Meta, and users who pay for software [12:47]. He also breaks down why TikTok's overseas ad efficiency lags Meta/Instagram/YouTube: ad models need massive scale, long engagement (Douyin averages over an hour daily, about 30 ads per 100 videos watched), and user data - which Apple's tracking restrictions crippled in the US, which is why OpenAI is betting on hardware [15:18].

Bloome's core philosophy: "from day one with Bloome, we treated agents as people" [24:21]. Agents need social graphs and memory built from their network, not just screen capture, echoing Marx: "Man is the ensemble of social relations" [25:11]. In their own code-repo chat, "zero human-to-human @mentions now" [21:53] - you just @ an agent to fix a bug, and ten minutes later it is fixed and stakeholders are notified; he estimates "at least a 5x improvement" [23:20]. Beta-group user operations that once needed two interns now run on Bloome, with agents replying to users, investigating, fixing, and notifying [23:26]. Organizationally, treating agents as people turns the org into permission management [27:48], where "every company is essentially a file system" [29:10], and agents are either personal (permissions match their owner) or company-owned (task-based permissions). It also solves institutional memory: salespeople leave with their notebooks, and Google's internal saying is "it takes six months to truly understand your team" [27:00].

On monetization and strategy: "token-based pricing is inherently flawed" [37:37] - a million tokens spent on a tomato-scrambled-eggs recipe costs the same as a million tokens proving the Riemann Hypothesis with Terence Tao, while the value differs enormously; outcome-based pricing is inevitable, because "if everyone sticks to token-based pricing, no one will make money. Not even Anthropic" [39:22]. Future platforms will sell trust, the way Taobao's escrow did [39:36], since AI today bears no liability for outcomes (the Doubao mushroom cases [40:09]); Stripe shows how valuable that is - it "built a cult in Silicon Valley" [41:19]. On adoption, the blocker is organizational inertia, not technology: Slack won among teams "with zero legacy baggage" [35:11]. On moats, Kuaishou's retired CEO Su Hua warned against day-one defensiveness: "First build a castle, only then does a moat matter" [46:45]. For going global, start from pain points inside existing workflows like Shopify [30:04], build familiarity through consistent content [31:42], and remember "field sales will never disappear" [32:57].
