# What being a female startup founder in UK is like | London vs SF, Fundraising, Imposter Syndrome

Sean Chen sits down in London with two 24-year-old British founders: Maya, building Rebeauty, "Rebeauty is essentially Vinted, but for beauty" [01:33], a marketplace for reselling unopened, unused beauty products (PR, unwanted gifts, miss returns), and Victoria, building Ventureship, which she describes as "basically hiring reinvented" [01:56], hiring through open innovation projects instead of AI screenings and psychometric tests.

Their product theses both come from personal pain. Maya argues fashion marketplaces host beauty listings but with the wrong taxonomy: "if you're searching for a serum, you'd search for ingredients that you'd like, like vitamin C, retinol, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide" [04:24], plus morning/night usage and skin concerns like acne or rosacea; AI-personalized search is on her roadmap, but not for the first launch [05:40]. Victoria started Ventureship after watching friends' applications vanish into AI-screening voids, and she identified four problems: candidates cannot get jobs, and companies face higher chances of bad hires, time and cost of hiring, and all-consuming wrong hires [06:29]. She hires her own interns through take-home projects, and both founders insist AI is "my best friend in growing this startup" but cannot replace authentic, resonant human content on social platforms [12:41].

The London vs San Francisco contrast dominates the conversation. America "is so much more friendly and... much more startup friendly" [16:17], while the UK is risk-averse and resists people who do not fit a box. Victoria's SF accelerator trip left her feeling lazy by comparison: "I don't know what they drink over there. They've got rocket fuel in their veins" [18:06], with investors who brush off small raises as "less than my kitchen renovation" [18:53]. Her summary: "America's more in conviction rather than proof and the UK's more in proof because we're so risk averse" [19:06]. Sean's own story confirms it: Antler London rejected him while pushing projected revenue, and the US investors who later backed him called that same projection a red flag for a pre-seed company [19:26]. UK positives exist too: SEIS/EIS tax relief returns 70% of a £10,000 angel investment [23:01], and the female-founder ecosystem is growing fast through Barclays Eagle Labs, Female Founders Rise, and Let's Fund Her [23:43], including FFR's UK-wide campaign putting successful female founders on billboards under the message "we've done it, you can do this too" [24:45].

On founder psychology, the trio trades imposter-syndrome remedies: do not project other people's success or failure onto yourself [32:11], Naval's line that "when you do something that you feel like you're playing, you're winning" [32:46], "comparison is completely the thief of joy" [33:17], and Maya's dad's mantra that "the difference between a winner or a loser is a winner never gives up" [29:52], echoed by Odd Muse founder Amy's story of pushing through the point where others give up [30:03]. Overrated advice: "follow your heart" [61:21] - better to set a time limit, iterate, and move on [64:14]. Maya found her market as a customer who could not afford Dr. Barbara Sturm's £200 creams and discovered beauty resale on Depop, Vinted, and eBay [35:32]; her first buyer unpacked a makeup bag full of Vinted purchases on their first call [36:48]. Investor horror stories (a man putting his hand in Victoria's face at a networking event [51:11], Canva rejected 100 times by 100 VCs [49:26]) lead to practical rules: investors are humans with no special moat [55:21], and investor relationships "last longer than marriages" so pick ones who contribute [58:40].
