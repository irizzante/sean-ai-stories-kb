# AI Jason Podcast: AI products, YouTube(220k+), Jian-Yang, Startups

Sean Chen meets AI Jason (Jason Zhou), the Sydney-based YouTuber with 222,000 YouTube subscribers, 30.5k on X, and a 1,281-member AI Builder Club community [01:34], a former product designer turned AI builder and founder of superdesign.dev and trulyt.io [01:43]. The channel's origin story is the Jian-Yang joke: he put the Silicon Valley character's face on a thumbnail and "50% of people I think went in the video because they really thought Jian Yang is making AI videos" [01:04]. His background is a chain of pivots: power engineering in Chongqing (he wanted nuclear but "Chinese university would mainly teach you how to burn coal" [02:52]), product design, a move to Sydney for his girlfriend, joining SafetyCulture where he watched it grow from 50 to 700 people into an Australian unicorn [03:29], then a first startup born from a Binance-funded hackathon [04:20]. The GPT-3.5 API hooked him into AI [04:31], and he started the YouTube channel to keep learning momentum [05:09]; his first video went up May 4, 2023, with an earlier Kanye West AI-voice-clone experiment in Google Colab [05:33].

His content philosophy contrasts the two dominant creator types - hyper-technical or hype-driven news - by building prototypes instead: try the new technology, build a proof of concept, and show viewers "not only understand the concept but also know what they can do about it" [06:04]. Sean himself learned LangChain and LangGraph from Jason's videos [07:00]. Videos grew from the 5-minute playbook to 20-60 minutes because audiences want depth [08:04]. On hype cycles: "if you are in AI, every day you will feel like AGI is arrived and your business is going to die. But if you just take a break in the mountain for a month and you come back you realize everything is still the same" [36:41]; the founder's job is understanding the model's boundary and designing a golden path, a point he credits to the PM of Claude Code [37:21]. Editing a 30-minute video takes him about an hour [09:54], while AI video editing remains unsolved because video is three-dimensional information [10:36]. His one content rule: "you basically just need to make useful things" [43:27].

His products track his journey. Superdesign (first called Supernova) began as a weekend Cursor IDE extension with an infinite canvas for comparing design variants, "almost like Figma inside IDE" [16:24]; the open-source project hit 4,000 GitHub stars in a few months [16:26], he raised a first round in the US, and launched a cloud platform [16:44]. On the designer debate: roles are blurring into builders; he locked his design team in a room for a day to design inside the codebase [20:03], and they began shipping 3-5 PRs per week; "the designer who just design in Figma... their value is really decreasing very fast" [20:47], while taste "actually come from your unique experience that no one else have" [25:24]. Trulyt (trulyt.io) came from OpenClaw experiments automating support, growth, and ops [26:26]; a Cruise friend told him "team who use agent analytics is going to be 10x more effective" [27:09]. After connecting an agent to PostHog and the codebase, it found analytics-event gaps and feature-to-conversion correlations, and one day detected a signup spike, ran SQL, identified a single account farming referral credits, and reset those credits on its own [30:28]. The product pitch: "a platform to help next generation of AI native company to scale without hiring people" [31:37], with each agent running in its own sandbox with a browser and CLI [32:29].

His theses: long-running, self-improving agents with memory are the next wave [35:11], chat is a bad interface for agents because "it is very hard for you to understand what is actually going on" [34:37], every company will get its own Jarvis [39:52], and value will accrue to vertical open-core solutions per workflow [40:27]. On Australia: Canva and Atlassian changed the scene, Blackbird, AirTree, and Square Peg were the three early VCs, and the unicorns-per-million-raised ratio was "either number one or number two in the world" [12:41]; Antler told Sean the best portfolio companies come from Australia, Singapore, and Scandinavia [12:55], though US investors do not recognize Australian work experience [13:23]. On China: Hangzhou West station's numbered ride-share pickup system is "I've never seen this anywhere else in the world" [47:05], a Shanghai meetup posted two days in advance drew 40+ people, and "most Chinese people don't talk much on the internet, but they're like super hardcore" [48:36] - he recommends hiring there [48:45].
